Fabric selection is one of the most important decisions when developing a custom hoodie. The right material helps determine how the garment feels, drapes, performs, and fits into your brand's product range.

For fashion labels, streetwear brands, sportswear businesses, and clothing wholesalers, understanding fabric weight is particularly important. A lightweight hoodie designed for layering has different requirements from a heavyweight streetwear hoodie intended for colder weather.

One of the most useful specifications to understand is GSM. By learning how GSM works and how it interacts with fabric construction and composition, you can make better decisions before moving into sampling and production.

## 01. Understanding GSM for Hoodies

GSM stands for grams per square metre. It measures the weight of a fabric over a standard area of one square metre. In apparel manufacturing, GSM is commonly used to communicate fabric weight and compare material options.

When discussing **custom hoodie fabric weight**, GSM provides a useful starting point. However, it does not tell you everything about the fabric. Two materials with the same GSM can feel different because of their yarn, fiber composition, knitting structure, and finishing.

For this reason, GSM should be considered alongside fabric samples, composition details, and the intended garment design.

## 02. Hoodie Fabric Weight Guide

Hoodie fabrics are available in a range of weights. The following ranges are illustrative starting points, not fixed industry standards. Actual suitability depends on construction, finishing, and intended use.

| Fabric weight | Illustrative GSM range | Typical application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lightweight | Around 200–260 GSM | Layering and lighter hoodies |
| Midweight | Around 270–330 GSM | Everyday casual hoodies |
| Heavyweight | Around 340–400 GSM | Structured and warmer hoodies |
| Extra heavyweight | Above 400 GSM | Specialized heavyweight styles |

A midweight fabric may be a practical starting point for an everyday collection, while heavier options may be worth sampling for structured streetwear designs. Always assess the actual fabric before confirming specifications.

## 03. Fleece vs French Terry

Fleece and French terry are two common fabric options used in hoodie development. Both can be suitable for custom apparel, but their inner surfaces and overall characteristics differ.

![Close-up comparison of brushed fleece and French terry fabric textures](/images/blog/hoodie-gsm-comparison.webp)

### Fleece Fabric

Fleece commonly features a brushed inner surface. This creates a soft, warmer feel that can work well for winter collections, casual hoodies, and comfortable everyday garments.

### French Terry Fabric

French terry generally has looped yarns on the inside rather than a brushed surface. It can be a useful option for transitional clothing, lighter layers, and designs where a loopback texture is preferred.

Neither fabric is automatically better. The decision should reflect your product positioning, target season, desired hand feel, and design requirements.

## 04. Consider Fabric Composition

Fabric composition describes the fibers used to make the material. Cotton, polyester, and blended constructions can produce different characteristics.

Cotton is often selected for its natural feel, while polyester may be incorporated for different performance and durability requirements. Blends combine fibers to achieve a balance suited to the intended product.
When developing a private label hoodie, consider the composition together with GSM, fabric construction, shrinkage, color consistency, and finishing requirements.

## 05. How to Choose the Right Hoodie Fabric

Before finalizing a fabric specification, consider these important factors:

1. **Define your target customer.** Consider who will wear the hoodie and what they expect from its comfort, fit, and appearance.
2. **Identify the intended season.** A summer layering piece and a winter hoodie may require different fabric weights and constructions.
3. **Choose the desired silhouette.** Oversized and structured designs may need different fabric characteristics from lightweight fitted styles.
4. **Review fabric composition.** Compare fiber content, surface texture, and finishing alongside GSM.
5. **Evaluate physical samples.** Check hand feel, drape, stretch, recovery, and appearance before approving the material.

## 06. Common Fabric Selection Mistakes

Choosing fabric based only on GSM is a common mistake. Weight is important, but it does not independently determine softness, warmth, durability, or quality.

Another mistake is approving a fabric without checking how it behaves after washing. Shrinkage, color changes, pilling, and changes in surface appearance can affect the finished garment.

Brands should also avoid selecting materials without considering their target retail price and product positioning. A fabric specification should support the overall product concept, not just one specification.
